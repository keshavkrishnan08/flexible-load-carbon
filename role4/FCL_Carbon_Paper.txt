Flexible Computing Loads, Electricity Grid Resilience,
and Carbon Emission Reduction:
A Tale of Bitcoin Mining and AI Training

(Extremely preliminary working draft)
Abstract
We develop an analytical framework for flexible computing loads (fcls)—intensive
electricity demands that can scale elastically in real time (Bitcoin mining is an archetypal fcl; AI training an imperfect alternative). In a model of an integrated grid with
endogenous renewable and fossil capacity, we show that if the amortized capital cost of
fcl hardware falls below a threshold—a condition currently met by Bitcoin mining—
introducing fcls with demand response may generate a triple win: higher utility profit,
lower blackout probability, and lower carbon emissions. This occurs as fcls (1) incentivize renewable investment by monetizing otherwise uncompensated renewable surplus
and (2) reduce fossil usage by substituting for peak-hour fossil backups. We find these
benefits also prevail in deregulated competitive markets and are amplified under rateof-return regulation if fcl revenues are passed to consumers as lower retail prices.
Legacy capacity, however, may reverse the carbon-reduction effect in the short run.
Our analysis lays a foundation for evaluating the carbon-reduction potential of fcls
and provides guidance for designing demand response programs to harness them.

Keywords: AI, Bitcoin, blockchain, carbon emissions, demand response, electricity pricing
policy and regulation, flexible computing loads, mining, renewable energy, sustainability

Introduction

Bitcoin mining, and more broadly proof-of-work blockchain operations, have long been criticized for their high electricity consumption. Media headlines frequently compare Bitcoin
mining’s energy use to that of a mid-sized country (Forbes, 2022), and some studies have
made the bold claim that Bitcoin mining alone could contribute to a 2◦ C rise in global temperatures (Mora, Rollins, Taladay, Kantar, Chock, Shimada and Franklin, 2018). Some scholars
have even pointed to their policy analyses linking Bitcoin mining to carbon emissions as a primary justification for China’s ban of Bitcoin mining in September 2021.1 Given rising Bitcoin
prices, recent political shifts, and increasing adoption by governments and corporations—
including recent initiatives for strategic Bitcoin reserves (U.S. Senator Lummis, 2024; The
Washington Post, 2024) and the emergence of Bitcoin treasury companies—the energy demand from Bitcoin mining is likely to grow further, making a rigorous assessment of its
environmental impact more urgent than ever.
Meanwhile, the explosive growth of artificial intelligence has created an even larger
class of electricity-intensive computing operations. Training a frontier AI model today can
require tens of thousands of specialized processors running continuously for weeks, with
energy demands rivaling those of a mid-sized data center. Like Bitcoin mining, such batch
training workloads are elastically scalable: the GPU cluster can to a large extent be scaled
up or down in real time without disrupting later operations, so the jobs can in principle be
scheduled around grid conditions. This shared property—the ability to absorb or release
electricity demand in real time without disrupting later operations—defines what we call
flexible computing loads (fcls). Bitcoin mining is the archetypal fcl; AI training is a close
but imperfect cousin. Developing a rigorous analytical framework for fcls as a class—rather
than treating Bitcoin mining and AI in isolation—is one of the contributions of this paper.

While it is indisputable that Bitcoin mining (and fcl in general) consumes vast amounts
of electricity, we argue that a closer analysis of its economic structure reveals a strikingly
different possibility. With appropriately designed demand response programs, the high electricity demand from Bitcoin mining may actually lower net carbon emissions. To see why,
note two characteristics of the electricity system that our model makes precise. First, renewable energy sources (e.g., wind or solar) feature high fixed costs but near-zero variable costs
and imperfect correlation between supply and demand. This supply-demand mismatch forces
grids to rely on fossil “peaker” plants for balancing, imposing carbon costs and undermining the commercial case for renewable investment. Second, Bitcoin mining is memoryless:
every joule of hashing contributes equally to expected rewards regardless of when it occurs,
so miners are perfectly indifferent between running now and running later. This temporal
flexibility—unique among large electricity consumers—makes Bitcoin mining an ideal shock
absorber for intermittent renewable supply. Mining can be dialed up during renewable surplus (earning revenue on power that would otherwise be curtailed) and dialed down during
scarcity (freeing fossil capacity for consumers), simultaneously raising the profitability of
renewable investment and reducing the need for fossil backup.
It is important to stress that this carbon-reduction effect is not automatic. It requires that the demand response program be appropriately designed, and it depends on key
parameters—most importantly the amortized hardware cost of mining equipment relative
to the expected per-megawatt revenue from flexible dispatch. Our paper characterizes these
conditions precisely. When they are satisfied, mining generates a triple win: lower carbon
emissions, lower blackout probability, and higher utility profit. When they fail, the reverse
holds. The even-handed characterization of both cases is, we argue, the central contribution
of a formal analysis that informal advocacy cannot provide.2

Some practitioners and policy advocates have argued informally that Bitcoin mining can act as a flexible
grid resource (Ibañez and Freier, 2023; Bitcoin Policy Institute, 2024), and sporadic empirical anecdotes
support this channel (Forbes, 2024). Our paper provides the analytical foundation for evaluating when and

This paper develops the first rigorous analytical framework for studying these questions.
Unlike conventional electricity uses—heating, lighting, industrial processes—where curtailment imposes an immediate service disruption, fcls can be elastically scaled up or down in
real time without disrupting later operations.
Our baseline model features a regulated electricity system in which a profit-maximizing
utility jointly decides how much renewable capacity, fossil backup capacity, and fcl capacity
to install. The key trade-off is between the high fixed cost of renewable capacity (which
generates electricity at zero marginal cost but intermittently) and the high variable cost of
fossil backup (which generates reliably but emits carbon). We show that integrating fcls
via a demand response program solves this trade-off through two reinforcing channels. First,
fcls monetize renewable surplus that would otherwise be curtailed, raising the revenue
from renewable investment and thus incentivizing more capacity. Second, fcls substitute
for fossil backup during hours when renewables just fall short of consumer demand, reducing
the installed fossil capacity at the optimum. Together, these channels can lower total carbon
emissions while increasing utility profit.
We characterize the exact conditions under which the profit and reliability gains occur
and show that they coincide; the carbon reduction obtains under additional distributional
conditions that we also characterize precisely. The mining preference condition takes the
intuitive form that the amortized capital cost of fcl hardware must be below the expected
per-megawatt value of flexible dispatch, which equals the per-MWh profit margin times the
fraction of hours the grid has spare fossil capacity.
Beyond the baseline model, we analyze four extensions that are critical for external validity and policy relevance. First, we consider timing variations: many grids have legacy
fossil or renewable capacity that is not freely adjustable. We show that the carbon improvement channel critically relies on endogenous fossil capacity choice. In the short run,

when both fossil and renewable capacities are fixed (Case C), the demand response program unambiguously increases carbon: FCLs dispatch on spare fossil power, raise the plant’s
utilization rate, and no capacity substitution offsets this effect. Critics who observe high
fossil utilization rates at mining facilities sited near gas plants are empirically correct—for
those legacy-capacity environments. Our result is that this short-run carbon cost is the flip
side of the long-run triple win: the same FCL revenue that increases fossil utilization in
the short run finances the renewable capacity expansion that reduces both fossil utilization
and carbon in the long run. A renewable-only dispatch constraint (capping FCL dispatch
at the realized renewable surplus (−x̃)+ ) eliminates even the short-run carbon cost, at the
expense of narrowing the FCL operating window and reducing the program’s profitability.
Second, we embed the model in the Averch–Johnson rate-of-return regulation framework,
where the retail electricity price is determined endogenously. Under ROR regulation, mining
revenue creates a “double win”: in addition to the standard capacity subsidy channel, it
is passed through to consumers as a lower retail electricity price. Third, we analyze deregulated competitive electricity markets, showing that the competitive equilibrium replicates
the regulated outcome with the market’s scarcity price playing the role of the regulated
penalty, and that fcls alleviate the missing money problem facing renewable generators by
converting zero-price surplus hours into fossil-marginal hours at price c, inducing additional
renewable investment KR∗ > KR◦ . Fourth, we examine the scope of the grid: whether the
model describes a price-taking regional grid or one with market power in the global hash
market, and how coordination failures arise when many grids simultaneously adopt demand
response programs.
Finally, we quantify the model’s predictions using current market data. Prevailing Bitcoin prices, reward per block (the “coinbase” reward), and falling renewable capital costs
imply that the viable range for fcl adoption is currently roughly 2.5 times wider than under
2022 conditions. For AI training, the viable hardware cost range depends critically on the

curtailable fraction θ and training revenue RAI : under plausible frontier-model assumptions
(θ = 0.5, RAI ≈ $200–400/MWh), the threshold is comparable to or wider than Bitcoin’s
current threshold. These quantitative assessments suggest that the carbon-reduction potential of fcls is not just a theoretical possibility but a practical reality under current market
conditions.
Key contributions. This paper makes three contributions to the intersection of energy
economics, blockchain, and operations management.
First, we provide the first analytical characterization of fcls as a grid resource, unifying
Bitcoin mining and AI training within a common framework that identifies when and why
flexible computing loads can reduce carbon emissions. Previous work on demand response
(Aïd, Possamaï and Touzi, 2022; Fattahi, Dasu and Ahmadi, 2023) does not consider the
specific properties of computing loads (memoryless, globally competitive, transparent revenue), and previous work on Bitcoin mining (Halaburda and Yermack, 2023; Cong, He and
Li, 2021) does not develop a full welfare analysis within an electricity system model.
Second, we provide tractable comparative-static results across a wide range of market
structures—regulated utilities, rate-of-return regulation, and competitive markets—enabling
a unified policy analysis of fcl programs across different electricity system designs.
Third, we identify and formalize the key differences between Bitcoin mining and AI
training as fcl technologies, including the inference latency constraint, revenue opacity,
and power surge behavior, providing a theoretic foundation for the emerging debate about
whether data centers can serve as flexible grid resources (Knittel, Senga and Wang, 2025).

Related Literature

Our work contributes to the intersection of four distinct streams of literature: research on
Bitcoin mining and blockchain, demand response programs, renewable energy investments
and electricity markets, and the economics of artificial intelligence and data centers.
Research on Bitcoin mining. On the theory side, Biais, Bisiere, Bouvard and Casamatta
(2019) model Bitcoin mining as a coordination game and identify the possibility of multiple
equilibria. Cong, He and Li (2021) studies the dynamics of mining pool formation and
shows that the rise of mining pools may lead to significant electricity consumption. Prat
and Walter (2021) propose a model that uses the Bitcoin–USD exchange rate to predict the
computing power of the network. Arnosti and Weinberg (2022) show that Bitcoin mining has
a tendency toward significant centralization given even small initial differences in variable
costs. Bertucci, Bertucci, Lasry and Lions (2024) models Bitcoin mining as a mean-field
game.
On the empirical side, the most directly related paper is Halaburda and Yermack (2023),
who study the operations and financial valuations of 13 cryptocurrency mining companies
listed on NASDAQ and find that miners using Texas wind power are offline more than
other miners in a more erratic pattern, yet are more profitable due to extremely low prices

10

during renewable surplus periods. Their findings directly support the mechanism we model.
Using Bitcoin prices as an instrument, Benetton, Compiani and Morse (2023) find that
cryptomining entry raises electricity prices for nearby households and small businesses in
upstate New York. Unlike our paper, the setting that leads to their finding does not feature
demand response programs that curtail demand during peak hours, a key economic force
behind our results.
Several industry reports also provide supporting evidence for our model. Ibañez and
Freier (2023) contain numerous verbal arguments on why Bitcoin mining may be environmentally friendly that echo key aspects of this paper. Bitcoin Policy Institute (2024) document data patterns consistent with Bitcoin mining’s flexible electricity demand. There is
also extensive anecdotal evidence of grids using Bitcoin mining to absorb surplus electricity,
including documented cases in Texas, Pakistan, and Alberta.
The flexibility of Bitcoin mining at the hardware level is grounded in ASIC design. Miners have been reported to switch operations “at a minute’s notice” (Bloomberg, 2021; CNBC,
2022; Forbes, 2021), and technical product manuals (BITMAIN Support, 2022) confirm that
rapid on/off cycling is a design feature. Yaish, Tochner and Zohar (2022) show that miners
can repeatedly switch machines to manipulate difficulty in ways that increase profits, confirming that rapid cycling is economically feasible. Yaish and Zohar (2023) further document two
firms—Layer1 Technologies (2022) and Blockstream (2022)—that designed ASICs explicitly
as grid shock absorbers, underscoring that hardware-level flexibility is an intentional design
feature rather than an artifact. Before Bitcoin mining was banned in China, miners routinely
migrated between hydro-rich regions as the wet and dry seasons shifted renewable availability
(Bitcoin.comNews, 2019; decrypt, 2020; Cointelegraph, 2020)—an early large-scale demonstration of FCL demand flexibility in practice. Kawaguchi, Noda and Komiyama (2022)
provide econometric evidence that miners adjust their electricity consumption in response
to profitability shocks, confirming elastic demand response in the field.

Research on electricity markets and renewable investment. Electricity supply has
historically been treated as a natural monopoly subject to rate-of-return (ROR) regulation,
under which utilities recover prudently incurred costs plus a regulated return on invested
capital (Train, 1991; Jamison, 2008). The landmark pathology of this regime is the Averch–
Johnson overcapitalization effect (Averch and Johnson, 1962): because allowed profits scale
with the rate base, regulated firms over-invest in capital relative to the social optimum.
This observation directly informs our Section 6.1. The theoretical critique of cost-of-service
regulation motivated the development of incentive regulation mechanisms; the landmark
contribution is Laffont and Tirole (1986), which formalizes the regulator’s problem as a
principal-agent relationship and shows that optimal contracts must trade off productive
efficiency against information rents captured by the firm. Abito (2020) estimates structurally
that moving to optimal incentive regulation could reduce costs by 20–30 percent for some
utilities.
Beginning in the 1990s, dissatisfaction with ROR regulation motivated electricity sector restructuring: separating generation from transmission, introducing bid-based wholesale
markets, and creating independent system operators (Joskow, 1997, 2003). Evidence on restructuring’s efficiency effects is largely positive (Fabrizio, Rose and Wolfram, 2007; Davis
and Wolfram, 2012; Cicala, 2022): Cicala (2015) shows that ROR regulation inflates fuel
procurement costs by roughly 10 percent relative to competitive procurement, and Abito,
Han, Houde and Van Benthem (2024) identifies the specific channels—removal of cost passthrough provisions, repositioning in input markets, and unwinding of legacy contracts—
through which divestiture raised plant efficiency. Market power and its impact on electricity
prices remain a concern, however (Wolak, 2003; Hortaçsu and Puller, 2008; Bushnell, Mansur
and Saravia, 2008; MacKay and Mercadal, 2024).
Electric Power Research Institute (2024) project significant near-term load growth from
data centers and identify grid reliability risks. Complementing the operational analysis,
12

Van Nieuwerburgh (2026) characterizes the financial architecture of this expansion, noting
that the massive scale of AI infrastructure is driving a shift toward third-party ownership
and private credit financing. Gete and Mercader (2026) document the emerging datacenter
mortgage market and analyze originate-to-distribute incentives that arise when lenders securitize data center loans, raising questions about underwriting standards in an asset class
whose long-run viability depends on sustained electricity availability. Satchwell, Mims Frick,
Cappers, Sergici, Hledik, Kavlak and Oskar (2025) survey large-load tariffs—real-time pricing, demand charges, and interruptible service agreements—through which utilities manage
financially and operationally challenging loads; the NARUC rate design primer (Abal, Hedman, Butterworth and Kneeland, 2021) provides the foundational cost-allocation framework
underpinning these instruments and is directly relevant for fcl program design.
Among academic work on renewable investment, Aflaki and Netessine (2017) show that
carbon pricing may sometimes discourage renewable adoption due to intermittency challenges. Kök, Shang and Yücel (2018) show that flat pricing can encourage solar adoption.
Kök, Shang and Yücel (2020) find that renewable and flexible energy sources complement
each other, whereas inflexible sources act as substitutes. Peng, Wu and Souza (2024) study
the interplay between renewable generation and energy storage. Andonov and Rauh (2024)
document the increasing entry of private equity in U.S. electricity markets, especially renewable investment. Bai and Wu (2023) find that private equity buyouts of fossil fuel power
plants reduce output-scaled CO2 and NOx emissions by improving production efficiency.
Darmouni, Lehrer and Sastry (2025) point out that the high fixed cost of renewables justifies ex-ante power purchase agreements. These papers provide context for understanding
fcls as a complementary mechanism for enabling renewable investment.
Research on demand response programs. A stream of research has explored demand
response programs in electricity markets. Peura and Bunn (2015) examine how peak electric-

ity producers set prices under uncertain demand. Aïd, Possamaï and Touzi (2022) analyze
optimal demand response contracts. Fattahi, Dasu and Ahmadi (2023) propose a scenariobased approach to optimize direct load control contracts. Lohmann and Rebennack (2017)
develop a long-term power generation expansion model that factors in short-term demand
response. Agrawal and Yücel (2022) investigate the role of baseline adjustments in demandresponse programs. Together, these studies highlight the importance of demand response
in balancing supply and demand, improving grid stability, and reducing costs. However,
none examines how such programs can leverage the specific properties of computing loads
to facilitate renewable investment—the central contribution of this paper.
Research on carbon pricing and externalities. Our analysis of carbon taxes and
fcl programs as complementary instruments (Section 6.2) connects to the canonical literature on environmental externalities. The Pigouvian case for carbon taxation dates to
Pigou (1920); its application to electricity markets was formalized in Baumol and Oates
(1988) and extended to intermittent renewables by Fischer and Newell (2008), who show
that renewable portfolio standards and carbon taxes interact non-trivially when generation
is stochastic. Acemoglu, Aghion, Bursztyn and Hemous (2012) study directed technical
change under environmental constraints, showing that carbon taxes can tilt innovation toward clean technologies—a mechanism that parallels the fcl investment channel we model.
Ambec and Crampes (2012) analyze the complementarity between renewable energy and
flexible backup generation, providing theoretical foundations closely related to our capacity
substitution result. The empirical evidence on carbon pricing is reviewed in Goulder and
Schein (2013): while carbon taxes are first-best in theory, second-best instruments including
renewable subsidies (Investment Tax Credits, Production Tax Credits) and demand response
programs are often more politically feasible. Our paper provides a foundation for evaluating
fcl demand response as one such second-best instrument, characterizing precisely when it

substitutes for—and when it complements—explicit carbon pricing.
Research on AI, data centers, and electricity. The electricity demands of artificial intelligence have received growing attention. Electric Power Research Institute (2024) project
that U.S. data centers will require 90–100 GW of additional generation capacity by 2030,
placing AI on a scale comparable to current Bitcoin mining. International Energy Agency
(2024) document that global data centers consumed approximately 415 TWh in 2024, and
project this to exceed 800 TWh by 2030 under a high-growth scenario. Feher, GarciaAppendini and Mihet (2025) provide evidence that U.S. data center development has been
substantially subsidized by public resources, raising questions about efficient capacity allocation and the distributional implications of concentrating energy-intensive compute in specific
localities. Gargano and Giacoletti (2026) document the scope of U.S. state tax incentives for
data center siting, finding that interstate competition has generated large public subsidies
for an industry whose energy demands are increasingly material to grid planning—a policy
context directly relevant to the demand response programs we analyze.
The flexibility of AI training loads has attracted emerging interest. Modern distributed
training frameworks support checkpointing—pausing mid-run without losing progress—which
is directly analogous to Bitcoin miners powering down ASICs and restarting (Jiang, Lin
et al., 2024). Hyperscalers (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) have begun using temporal flexibility to reduce energy costs and participate in demand response programs (Acun et al., 2023;
Radovanović, Koningstein et al., 2023). The feasibility of demand-response participation by
data centers is documented in Texas, where ERCOT’s large flexible load program includes
multiple data center operators alongside Bitcoin miners (Knittel, Senga and Wang, 2025).
The key difference between AI training and Bitcoin mining lies in AI inference: serving live queries is latency-constrained in ways that hashing is not, reducing the curtailable

3

Model

This section presents the overall model setup, which captures a regulated electricity system with stochastic renewable generation, fossil backup, and an optional demand response
program that integrates flexible computing loads.
Our baseline model has one strategic decision maker: a regulated utility. The utility
chooses three capacity levels—renewable capacity KR , fossil backup capacity KF , and flexible computing load capacity KM (subscripts R, F , M denote renewable, fossil, and mining,
respectively)—to maximize expected profit. The utility’s profit is then obtained from revenues from consumer electricity sales and computing revenue, minus variable fossil costs,
blackout penalties (which the regulator requires it to internalize), and capital costs for all
three capacity types. In the baseline model the utility sets KM directly (in practice, KM
may be implemented through a demand response program that contracts with third-party
miners; we model it as a direct capacity choice for simplicity); we relax this assumption
in Appendix E. The regulator sets the retail price p exogenously and imposes a blackout
penalty β. In the timing variations (Section 5), p remains exogenous; we endogenize it under
rate-of-return regulation (Section 6.1) and in deregulated spot markets (Section 6.2).

the scope of this paper. Appendix D will separately analyze the case R ≤ c.

Remark 1 (Revenue transparency and self-enforcement). Lemma 1 relies on two conditions
from Assumption 1: R > c (FCL is always worth dispatching on spare fossil capacity) and
β + p > R (FCL always yields to consumers during scarcity). Crucially, verifying R > c in
real time requires that R be publicly observable. Bitcoin mining satisfies this transparency
requirement by construction: R equals the block reward value divided by global network
energy per block, both of which are published continuously by the Bitcoin protocol. The
dispatch rule is therefore self-enforcing—both the utility and the miner can independently
verify that the margin is positive, eliminating the need for costly monitoring or revelation
mechanisms. AI training lacks a transparent real-time analogue of R, complicating the design
of an incentive-compatible dispatch contract (see Section 8).

Endogenous Electricity Prices

The baseline result in Section 4 specifies an exogenous electricity price p to regular (non-fcl)
customers. In reality, however, electricity prices are typically endogenously determined (at
least in the United States) either through (1) rate-of-return regulation (in regulated markets
such as most southern states) or (2) competitive bidding (in deregulated markets such as
California, Texas, Illinois, and many northeastern states). This section studies how our main
insight survives different market structures.

Despite potential carbon reduction benefits from increased renewable capacity buildup, our
analysis above also shows that fcl programs directly increases fossil dispatch during sparecapacity hours. The standard policy instrument for internalizing this externality is a carbon
tax on fossil output. Because fcl profitability depends on the spot price during fossildispatch hours, a carbon tax interacts directly with the FCL participation decision—raising
the effective cost of electricity for fcl operators and potentially pushing computing toward
zero-carbon hours. We now examine how this interaction shapes the joint design of carbon
pricing and fcl programs.
A carbon tax τ per unit of fossil output raises the effective marginal cost to c+τ , shifting
the spot price during dispatch. The carbon tax thus interacts with fcls in two ways:
1. Direct effect: The higher dispatch price (c + τ ) reduces the fcl profit margin to
R − (c + τ ) during fossil dispatch hours. If τ > R − c, fcl operators shut down
whenever fossil is the marginal generator, and compute only on renewable surplus
(x̃ ≤ 0). This endogenously tilts computing toward zero-carbon hours without an
explicit renewable mandate.
2. Indirect effect: The carbon tax shrinks the fossil scarcity margin from Pcap − c to
45

Pcap − (c + τ ), reducing fossil revenue during scarcity events. This discourages fossil
entry and raises the equilibrium blackout probability (as confirmed by ϕ◦ (τ ) decreasing
in τ in (38)). A higher carbon tax therefore partially unwinds the reliability gain that
fcl programs provide in the regulated baseline.
Carbon taxes and fcl demand response programs are therefore complementary instruments:
a sufficiently high τ pushes computing exclusively onto renewable surplus, while tax revenues
can fund renewable capacity subsidies. Note, however, that for τ ≥ τ̄ = R − c, fcl operates
only at price 0 (during renewable surplus) and no longer keeps fossil dispatched during
formerly zero-price hours. The renewable missing money channel identified above—whereby
fcl demand converts surplus hours into fossil-marginal hours at price c, inducing KR∗ > KR◦ —
is therefore eliminated, and equilibrium renewable investment KR∗ reverts toward KR◦ .

Grid Scope: Local versus Global Mining

Throughout Sections 4–6.2, we have treated the per-MWh mining revenue R as exogenous
to the grid operator. This is reasonable: electricity utilities serve geographically distinct
regions, while Bitcoin mining revenue is determined by the global hash rate—an aggregate
of computing loads across thousands of independent grids worldwide. For any single utility,
changes in its fcl program have negligible effect on R: a 100 MW program represents
roughly 0.6% of the ∼16 GW global mining fleet as of 2025 (and even a 1 GW program only
∼6%).
This section asks what changes as the scope of adoption grows. Section 7.1 considers the
polar extreme: a single utility that serves all miners worldwide, so that R is fully endogenous
to its choices. The fcl program collapses in that case—expanding capacity merely dilutes
the per-unit reward proportionally, leaving total mining revenue unchanged. Section 7.2
analyzes the intermediate case of N regional utilities each adopting independently: each
individually remains too small to affect R, so the baseline analysis applies utility by utility,
but collectively their programs reduce R for everyone, creating a commons externality that
places outcomes between the baseline (full adoption, negligible aggregate effect) and the
global monopolist (zero adoption). Throughout this section, we will use Bitcoin mining as
an example for fcl and model around its specific features.

Carbon accounting and the miner-relocation concern. A natural objection is that
local carbon reductions may be illusory: if a grid’s fcl program attracts miners from highercarbon regions, the grid’s own fossil investment falls but global emissions may be unchanged
or worse. Three features of the Section 4 analysis address this concern. First, the carbon
reduction operates through the capacity substitution channel: the utility builds more renewables because fcl revenue raises their shadow value (Proposition 1, Step 3). This investment
is a local, durable decision that reduces the grid’s carbon intensity regardless of where miners
eventually locate. Second, once renewable capacity is installed it is sunk—subsequent miner
relocation does not reverse it. Third, the carbon benefit is largest in grids with high existing
renewable penetration (Section 5.2), precisely the grids most likely to attract renewableseeking miners, so the relocation effect reinforces rather than undermines the carbon case.
No cross-jurisdictional coordination is required; the mechanism is self-contained within a
single grid’s investment decision.
The two polar cases—price-taking local grid and a single global grid operating all
mining—bracket the range of realistic scenarios, with the multi-grid Nash equilibrium falling
in between: individually rational adoption that is collectively excessive. Our baseline analysis
covers the price-taking case; the global grid case is analyzed in Appendix C.

curtailment incurs no adjustment costs.

8.2

AI training as an emerging FCL

We now analyze AI training as a potential fcl, in comparison to Bitcoin mining.
Similarities. Large-language-model (LLM) training runs last days to weeks and support
checkpointing: pausing mid-run without losing progress, then resuming— analogous to powering down ASICs and restarting. A 50-hour annual curtailment (Duke Energy’s estimated
threshold for accommodating large data center loads without new generation) is imperceptible in a three-week training run. Hyperscalers (Google, Microsoft, Amazon) can also reroute
training workloads across data centers in different grid regions, providing spatial demand
response unavailable to geographically fixed Bitcoin miners.
At scale, AI compute is comparable to Bitcoin mining: global data centers consumed
approximately 415 TWh in 2024, projected to exceed 800 TWh by 2030 (IEA). U.S. AIfocused data centers alone are forecast to require 90–100 GW of additional capacity by 2030,
comparable to Bitcoin’s current 16 GW continuous draw.13
The inference latency constraint. The most important structural difference between AI
and Bitcoin mining is the inference workload. AI inference—serving live queries to products

most importantly, a full life-cycle analysis would need to assess not just the hardware but
the counterfactual: would those semiconductors have been manufactured anyway for other
uses? If demand for ASICs is inframarginal to semiconductor production, their carbon attribution is near zero. We leave a formal life-cycle assessment to future work and flag this
as a limitation of the current flow-carbon analysis.

Conclusion

This paper has developed and analyzed an analytical model of flexible computing loads
(FCLs) as a mechanism for reducing carbon emissions through demand response in electricity markets. The core result is that, under economically plausible conditions, integrating
FCLs via a demand response program simultaneously increases utility profit, reduces carbon
emissions, and enables more renewable capacity investment—a triple win that critically relies
on the capacity substitution channel.
In sum, our main analytical results can be summarized as follows.
In the baseline simultaneous optimization, the mining preference condition CM < C̄M =
(R − c)ϕ◦ is both necessary and sufficient for the profit and reliability gains; the carbon
reduction additionally requires the distributional conditions characterized in Proposition 4
(for example, it holds under the uniform distribution when renewable capital costs are sufficiently low). The threshold C̄M is informative: it equals the per-MWh profit margin times
the probability of non-blackout, reflecting the expected revenue per MW of computing hardware.
Timing variations reveal that the carbon improvement channel critically depends on
the endogeneity of fossil capacity. A crucial caveat is that when both fossil and renewable capacities are fixed in the short run (Case C), the demand response program raises
carbon without exception. FCLs dispatch on spare fossil power, increasing the plant’s utilization rate, and no capacity-substitution force offsets this effect (formally, ∆Carbon =

Rate-of-return regulation introduces a double win: computing revenue reduces the required retail electricity price under ROR pass-through, benefiting consumers directly in
addition to subsidizing renewable capacity. The Averch–Johnson over-investment in capital
inadvertently benefits consumers through higher reliability, and computing revenue amplifies
this effect.
Deregulated competitive markets replicate the regulated outcomes with the market’s
scarcity price replacing the regulatory penalty. Computing loads alleviate the missing money
problem facing renewable generators—by converting zero-price surplus hours into fossilmarginal hours at price c, they raise renewable revenue and attract additional renewable
investment—and are complementary to carbon taxes, which endogenously tilt computing
toward zero-carbon hours.
Comparing Bitcoin mining and AI training, Bitcoin mining dominates on the three
key flexibility dimensions: temporal flexibility (memoryless hashing), revenue transparency
(publicly verifiable block rewards), and hardware modularity (scalable from kilowatts to gigawatts). AI training potentially offers higher revenue and spatial rerouting flexibility, but
is constrained by inference latency and revenue opacity.
Quantitatively, current market conditions (Bitcoin near $95,000, post-halving rewards,
dramatically lower renewable CapEx) imply a viable range for FCL adoption approximately
2.5 times wider today than under 2022 conditions.

Policy implications. The analysis suggests several policy actions. Utility commissions
should consider streamlined rate treatment for demand response programs that integrate
FCL operators, particularly given the double win under ROR regulation. Regulators should
evaluate FCL program timelines over a multi-year horizon to allow the capacity substitution
channel to operate. Carbon tax levels above the mining margin R − c would endogenously
restrict computing to renewable surplus hours, a simpler and more robust mechanism than
prescriptive renewable-only dispatch requirements. Finally, given the difficulty of verifying
AI training revenues, blockchain-based computing (Bitcoin mining or similar transparentrevenue loads) is currently the more practical FCL candidate for publicly accountable demand response programs.
Limitations and future research. Several important extensions remain. First, stochastic BTC prices (correlated or uncorrelated with electricity prices) introduce real-options
considerations in both the fcl investment decision and the renewable capacity choice. Second, multi-grid competition with endogenous hash-rate pricing creates a strategic interaction
not captured in the price-taking model. Third, a continuous-time model with capital adjustment costs and learning curves would capture the dynamic aspects of the renewable
transition. Fourth, empirical validation—particularly using ERCOT panel data on Bitcoin
miners’ curtailment behavior and grid outcomes—would provide direct tests of the model’s
comparative-static predictions.

C.6

Policy implication

In the Nash equilibrium each grid over-adopts relative to the coordinated optimum because
it ignores the negative externality its expansion imposes on other grids’ revenues. An international coordination mechanism (e.g., agreed limits on FCL program sizes) could improve
on the Nash outcome. The price-taking assumption in the main text is accurate for any
single grid in the current environment; as FCL programs proliferate, this assumption should
be revisited.

D

The Case R ≤ c

The main text assumes R > c (computing revenue per MWh exceeds fossil variable cost),
ensuring that FCL operators willingly consume fossil electricity. This appendix analyzes the
boundary case R = c and the unlikely case R < c.
92

D.1

Case R = c

When R = c, the FCL operator is exactly indifferent between running on fossil electricity
and not running during fossil-dispatch hours. The FOC for KM (11) becomes (R − c) · F =
0 = CM , which is satisfied only if CM = 0 (free ASIC hardware). In practice, ASIC hardware
always has positive cost, so R = c implies no FCL investment. The demand response program
reduces to a pure renewable surplus absorption mechanism (FCLs run only when x̃ < 0),
which has no effect on fossil capacity decisions but increases renewable investment.

D.2

Case R < c

When R < c, computing is unprofitable during fossil dispatch hours. No profit-maximizing
FCL operator would accept electricity priced at c or above. The FCL program can still

Alternative Regulatory Objectives

The main text assumes a profit-maximizing regulated utility that internalizes a blackout
penalty imposed by the regulator. This appendix considers three alternative regulatory
objective functions that are common in practice: (1) minimizing the probability of blackout,
(2) minimizing the retail electricity price, and (3) maximizing social welfare.

F.1

Objective 1: Minimizing blackout probability

Suppose the regulator (or the utility under explicit mandate) aims to minimize blackout
probability Pr(x̃ > KF ; KR ) = 1 − F (KF ; KR ) subject to a profit constraint Π ≥ Π̄.
Without FCL. Maximizing F (KF ; KR ) subject to the profit constraint yields a corner
solution: the utility invests the maximum fossil capacity that breaks even. The resulting
blackout probability is lower than under profit maximization for any given KR , since reliability is the direct objective.
97

With FCL. Introducing the FCL program changes the profit constraint: computing revenue relaxes it, allowing a higher KF (more fossil backup) to be built while remaining
profitable. The FCL program therefore reduces blackout probability through two channels:
(i) direct demand reduction during scarcity events (FCLs shut down when x̃ > KF , exactly analogous to the baseline), and (ii) indirect capacity expansion (higher permitted fossil
investment from the relaxed profit constraint).
Proposition 7 (Blackout-minimizing objective). Under a blackout-minimizing objective with
binding profit constraint, allowing FCLs unambiguously reduces blackout probability whenever
CM < C̄M . The threshold C̄M is unchanged from Proposition 1.
Proof. The regulator minimizes 1 − F (KF ; KR ) subject to the utility earning at least a fixed
profit Π̄. At the constraint boundary, the objective equals 1 − F (KF ; KR ) evaluated at
the profit-constrained optimum. Introducing FCLs relaxes the profit constraint by adding

> 0 and

the program is profitable). The relaxed constraint allows the regulator to demand higher

Endogenous hash rate as a stabilizer. Table 23 understates the resilience of R to
price declines, because hash rate responds endogenously to BTC price: when prices fall,
less efficient miners exit, reducing global energy consumption per block and thereby raising
R relative to the fixed-hash-rate calculation. In bear markets, network difficulty adjusts
downward within two weeks, partially offsetting the price decline in R. Empirically, R has
historically exhibited a coefficient of variation roughly 40–50% lower than the BTC price
itself over multi-month horizons, making the mining margin R − c considerably more stable
than raw price volatility would suggest.
Implications for triple-win robustness (corrected). Three conclusions follow from
this sensitivity analysis. First, the model’s qualitative predictions are robust for any BTC
price above roughly $45,000 (post-halving): the triple-win threshold C̄M is positive. Whether
current ASIC hardware costs fall within the viable range depends on lifecycle and specific
hardware price: at $1.5M/MW and a 3-year lifecycle, CM ≈ $57/MWh just clears the
$60/MWh threshold; at $2.1M/MW the same lifecycle gives CM ≈ $80/MWh, requiring
either a longer depreciation horizon (≥ 4 years) or lower hardware prices to satisfy CM <
C̄M . Second, the quantitative size of C̄M scales roughly linearly with the mining margin
R − c, so the welfare gains from FCL adoption grow as BTC prices rise—meaning the
program is self-reinforcing in bull markets. At BTC above $120,000 (C̄M ≈ $87/MWh), even
the $2.5M/MW–3-year scenario is clearly viable. Third, bear-market periods (BTC below
$45,000) represent a genuine sustainability concern: FCL operators would halt voluntary
curtailment since dispatching on fossil reduces their margins, weakening the demand response
channel. A well-designed FCL contract should therefore include price floors that guarantee
a minimum R payment to the FCL operator, insulating the demand-response commitment

Implications for AI data center loads

For AI training with curtailable fraction θ = 0.5 and training-compute revenue RAI =
$200/MWh (reflecting the commercial value of frontier model training), the effective margin
is θRAI − c = 0.5 × 200 − 45 = $55/MWh—roughly comparable to the current Bitcoin mining
margin and similarly near the threshold under the corrected hardware calibration. However,
at current inferred RAI values the opacity problem means this revenue cannot be directly
contracted, making practical Demand Response Program implementation with AI training
loads substantially more challenging than with Bitcoin mining.
